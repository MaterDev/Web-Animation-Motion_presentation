SL-11

canvas

Canvas has no scene graph. That one absence is the whole technique: it is why nothing erases itself, why cost is counted in calls rather than in objects, why the pointer has to be rebuilt by hand, why the surface can be read back, and why it can leave the main thread. Five sections, each pairing the consequence with a screen from ACETATE — a lacquer transfer desk that exists only here.

Every number on this sheet is measured on this machine, now. Timing brackets end at a named sync point — getImageData(0,0,1,1) — because Chrome records 2D commands and rasterises them later, so a bracket with no sync point measures command submission and not drawing. Figures at or near the clock floor are marked †; the floor itself is printed beside every table. performance.now() is clamped to 1 ms in Firefox and WebKit, so a figure marked † there is a bound, not a value. §1

Persistence

Nothing erases itself, because nothing was stored. A DOM node you move leaves nothing behind — the browser knows where it was. Canvas keeps no record of a mark once it is drawn, so the previous frame is still on the surface unless you remove it yourself. Clearing is a decision, and not clearing is a technique.

§2

Batching

Cost is calls, because there are no objects. There is no retained list of 50 000 things for the engine to optimise over — there is only the sequence of commands you issue, and the price is set by which commands those are. Two tables, because there are two questions: what the method costs on a provably identical picture, and what the mark costs once you let the picture change.

§3

Hit testing

The pointer is rebuilt by hand, because there are no nodes. No element means no :hover, no event target, no accessibility tree. The standard answer is a second surface nobody sees: draw every object again in a colour that is its identity, then read the one pixel under the cursor and decode it. One getImageData call, regardless of how many objects there are.

Explanatory graphic
Identity encoded into colour, and one pixel read back out of it. The index travels in the red and green channels; alpha separates a hit from the ground.

§4

Readback

The surface can be read back, uniquely on this spectrum. SVG can tell you where a node is; it cannot tell you what colour a pixel ended up. Canvas can, synchronously, for any region. That single capability is what makes the brandmark below resolve out of pixels rather than out of coordinates — and it is what turns a drawing surface into an input.

Explanatory graphic
The mark is rasterised to an off-screen canvas, read back with getImageData, and every opaque pixel above threshold becomes one particle's destination. No path data is authored here — change the face and the target set changes with it.
ACETATE · screen 4
§5

Off-thread

It can leave the main thread, because it needs no DOM. A canvas's control can be transferred to a worker once, irreversibly, and from then on it is drawn by a thread your layout, your parsing and your blocked for loop cannot touch. Block the main thread below and watch which counter keeps moving.

Explanatory graphic
Two threads, one timeline. The main thread's frames stop for the length of the block; the worker's are driven by the compositor and do not.

What this sheet is holding itself to

  1. Name the instrument, and its floor. Every printed figure carries the method that produced it. Where the instrument cannot resolve a value the sheet prints a bound, never a zero.
  2. A timing bracket needs a sync point. Chrome defers 2D rasterisation. The first version of §2 measured command submission and reported that 2 300× the drawn area was free. With the sync point it costs 2.2×, and that correction is why §2 has two tables instead of one confident number.
  3. A control must vary the thing the instrument might be missing. A mark-count control passes happily on a blind clock; only an area sweep catches it.
  4. Headless and headed can agree and both be wrong. They did, here. The agreement made a GPU explanation feel like a resolution.
  5. If the finding inverts, the sheet says so. Batching 50 000 marks into one path is slower than issuing 50 000 calls, which is the opposite of the folklore. It is stated on output proven pixel-identical, because that is where a counter-intuitive claim has to stand.

How the chrome was checked

The five ACETATE screens are generated from one template, so their chrome is one object rather than five copies somebody has to keep in step. Verified by cloning each chassis into a single fixed, integer-aligned slot — comparing them where they live measures where each landed on the device-pixel grid, not whether the chrome is the same — and screenshotting three regions of each:

  • Banner: 0 of 18 696 pixels differ, across all five.
  • Section rail: two of the four pairs byte-identical; the other two differ on about 1 300 pixels by at most 57 levels. Identical markup, so this is sub-device-pixel placement — the five units are different heights and land on the grid differently.
  • Transport: differs, and mostly by design. Suppressing the faceplate grain drops the difference from 58 000 pixels to 20 000 and the worst channel delta from 24 to 14, which says most of it is the machined finish: the grain is one continuous 300 px texture anchored at the top of the unit, so a taller unit shows a different part of it at the foot. That is what a real faceplate does.

The same run is the evidence for the rule above it. Nothing outside the working area moves; the timecode digits, the readouts and the traces are data and do.

Fiction, and what is not fiction

ACETATE is invented. No artist, label or release named on this sheet exists; the programme material is a stated mathematical construction, not a recording, and nothing here decodes or plays audio. The measurements are real.
