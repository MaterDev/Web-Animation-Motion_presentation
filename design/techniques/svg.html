svg

The first technique on this spectrum where the picture is made of elements rather than pixels. That single fact is what you are buying: the marks are in the DOM, so CSS styles them, the pointer hits them, a screen reader can be told what they are — and every one of them costs a node, which is also what you are paying. Every demo here is built as one fictional product, Good n’Healthy, because a technique shown on a coloured square teaches nothing about when to reach for it.

THE TRADE

what a node buys, and what it costs

One decision explains most of this slide. Because every mark is a real element, CSS reaches it, the pointer hits it and a screen reader can be told what it is — and because every mark is a real element, the browser must style, lay out and paint each one. Both halves of that are demonstrated here rather than asserted, and the right-hand one is the whole reason the next slide exists. The graph is Good n’Healthy's food-group tree; the field beside it is what happens when you try to plot every logged item at once.

every mark is an element

A canvas hands you one element and a bag of pixels. SVG hands you a document. Hover a food group — the pointer hits the shape, not its bounding box, and nothing scripts that. The buttons restyle the whole graph from outside it, by putting one class on the root.

what a node costs

Each mark carries style resolution, layout participation and a paint record of its own. Drag the count up and watch where it stops being free. This is the honest reason Good n’Healthy plots a sample rather than every logged item — measured on your machine, not claimed.
