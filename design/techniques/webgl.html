SL-12

webgl

WebGL decides how things look. Every pixel below is the value of a function — of position, of the light, of the eye — evaluated when the frame is drawn and discarded when it is done. Nothing is stored. The picture does not exist until the program runs, and this sheet is three portraits of that fact, each a product somebody uses: a museum's interpretation panel, where the parameter is the lamp; a natural-history collection's animated plate, where it is the tilt; and a studio's identity system, where it is the seed. One object at a time, examined — and in each, the thing on screen could not have been drawn any other way.

The platform once tried to hand this stage to CSS. CSS Custom Filters (Adobe and WebKit, 2012–14) let a stylesheet attach a GLSL program to any element — and deliberately crippled the fragment stage: shaders used in custom CSS filters can't read DOM content texture pixels at all, and also they can't write to screen pixels directly, because a program that branches on the colour of a pixel it did not author can be timed, and timing leaks the pixel. WebGL answered the same threat the other way round: the shader may read anything, provided it is content you supplied — same origin, or CORS-approved. That is why the fragment shader lives behind a surface you feed yourself, and why the world below draws its own object rather than reading the page around it.

Every number on this sheet is measured on this machine, now. Timing brackets end at a named sync point — gl.readPixels(0,0,1,1) — because the GPU runs a frame or more behind the CPU, so a bracket with no sync point measures how fast commands were queued, not how long they took. performance.now() is clamped to 1 ms in Firefox and WebKit; a figure marked † there is a bound, not a value. WebGL's own GPU timer, EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2, is not used: it ships disabled or coarsened over timing-attack concerns, so the instrument here is coarser than the timestamp-query slide 14 will have — and that asymmetry is part of the difference between the two APIs, not an excuse for it. §1

Relief

The highlight travels, because the surface is evaluated, not stored. A photograph of a bronze holds one light. This panel holds the bronze — a height field, 1 048 576 samples of it — and computes what the lamp does to it every frame, from wherever the lamp is: a surface normal, a light vector and a view vector per pixel, and a march through the field to find out what stands in the light's way. Move the lamp and the relief appears and vanishes the way it does on a gallery floor, which is the one thing a picture of the object can never show. Put your pointer on the panel and the lamp is yours.

§2

Iridescence

The colour is stored nowhere, because it is computed from the angle. A jewel beetle's wing-case is not green. It is a stack of transparent plates a few hundred nanometres thick, and the green is what those plates do to white light arriving from one direction and leaving toward another: an interference, summed over every wavelength, at every point, for this eye. Change the angle and the colour moves toward blue — the published behaviour, and the one thing a photograph of the specimen gets wrong at every angle but the one it was taken at. There is no texture in the plate below. Tilt it.

Every pixel multiplies one 2×2 complex matrix per layer per wavelength per polarisation, so the per-pixel work scales with the layers and nothing else does. The 1-layer specimen is a different colour — thin, washed, a soap film — which is the point: the layers are not a cost knob, they are the animal. The hue shift is the model's own, from the same function that shades the pixels: the peak of the unpolarised reflectance at the crown, at 0° against 10° and at 30° against 40°. The literature gives the direction — shorter with incidence, indices 1.6–1.7 — and the sheet holds itself to that direction. It does not claim a cited magnitude.

§3

Season

The program is the artwork, because it has no resolution. A studio builds an identity system for a concert hall's season and hands it over; the client runs it. What the client runs is one fragment program: evaluated at 7016 × 9933 it is the A2 print file, evaluated at 1920 × 1080 sixty times a second it is the foyer screen, and the two cannot disagree because they are the same function at two sets of coordinates. The title is set into the motif through a field the motif computes — a dependent read, sampling one image at coordinates found in another, which nothing in CSS or SVG can do at all. Pick an event.

Figure 1, resolution. The A2 tile's program evaluated offscreen at 1×, 4× and 16× its area, bracketed to readPixels. Area is the control the canvas track proved a blind clock cannot fake: raster costs time and costs submission nothing. The ms-per-megapixel column is the number a print operator wants — it says what 7016 × 9933 will cost. Figure 2, the transfer — the number slide 14 prints its zero against. The mark is — particles integrated on the CPU and uploaded every frame with bufferSubData; WebGL has no compute stage to keep them on the GPU. The bracket opens before the upload and closes at a named sync point: a fenceSync polled with clientWaitSync inside the same task, and if the fence never signals inside the task — Chromium updates sync status between tasks — the bracket falls back to readPixels and says so. Control: bytes per frame, by padding the vertex stride at constant particle count, up to 31× the real frame's bytes — 248 B is the widest stride WebGL allows. The integration and the draw do not change; only the bytes do. A particle-count sweep would scale the CPU integration too and prove less than it appears to. The particle count is the contract with §3 of slide 14, which re-runs the same count with the CPU out of the loop.

What this sheet is holding itself to

  1. Name the instrument, and its floor. Every printed figure carries the method that produced it. Where the instrument cannot resolve a value the sheet prints a bound, never a zero.
  2. A timing bracket needs a sync point. The GPU runs behind the CPU by design; readPixels is the wall it cannot run past. A bracket without it measured how quickly a frame was described.
  3. A control must vary the thing the instrument might be missing. Here that is fragment work. Lamps multiply the per-pixel evaluation and nothing else — not the geometry, not the draw count, not an upload — so a reading that follows them is a reading of the shader.
  4. The audience sees the API. There is no Three.js on this sheet. The loader that was going to be borrowed was not needed once the object became a height field, and a material system would have put an abstraction between the room and the fragment program, which is the entire subject.
  5. Only the subject is shaded. Cover the medal and nothing that remains — frame, rule, dial, leader, label — has a highlight, a bevel or a shadow. The test for the medal itself is stricter than taste: move the light. A rendered surface responds the way the material would; a painting of one does not.

Fiction, and what is not fiction

The medal is invented. No such object, workshop or accession exists. The direction for this sheet asked for a CC0 scan; the Smithsonian's 3D host refused every non-browser client, and the per-object licence check the direction itself warned about is exactly the thing a hurried build gets wrong. So the object is cast in code when the page loads — a slab, a rim, seventy-two beads, an inscription set on an arc in the deck's own type, a sun, fourteen rays, six wave bands, a mould seam, a field of chasing, a scatter of casting pores and a patina — twelve named operations into one array, in about a third of a second. That array is a real height field, the lighting is a real calculation over it, and the measurements are real.

The specimen is a real species and an invented specimen. Chrysochroa fulgidissima, the Japanese jewel beetle, is the textbook case of multilayer interference in an elytron; the accession, the locality and the shape are authored. The refractive indices and the quarter-wave stack are the model in the literature, simplified to two alternating indices. The colours are computed, not chosen.

Lantern Hall does not exist. No such venue, season, event or studio. The identity system is real in the only sense that matters here: one program, four formats, a client-facing dial, and a specification spread. The measurements — including the transfer figure another sheet depends on — are real, and were taken on this machine when this page loaded.

A fourth portrait — a type specimen as a distance field — is specified and conditional; it is built only if it can show something native text cannot.
