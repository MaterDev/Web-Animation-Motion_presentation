SL-12

webgl

WebGL decides how things look. Every pixel below is the value of a function — of position, of the light, of the eye — evaluated when the frame is drawn and discarded when it is done. Nothing is stored. The picture does not exist until the program runs, and this sheet is three rooms built on that fact: a gallery of small arts-and-culture experiences on public assets, an encyclopaedia of structural colour, and a poster that is a program. Everything that moves here is WebGL — Three.js carries the loaders and the physically based materials, and where a program is the experience it is written out.

The platform once tried to hand this stage to CSS. CSS Custom Filters (Adobe and WebKit, 2012–14) let a stylesheet attach a GLSL program to any element — and deliberately crippled the fragment stage: shaders used in custom CSS filters can't read DOM content texture pixels at all, and also they can't write to screen pixels directly, because a program that branches on the colour of a pixel it did not author can be timed, and timing leaks the pixel. WebGL answered the same threat the other way round: the shader may read anything, provided it is content you supplied — same origin, or CORS-approved. That is why the fragment shader lives behind a surface you feed yourself, and why the world below draws its own object rather than reading the page around it.

Every number on this sheet is measured on this machine, now. Timing brackets end at a named sync point — gl.readPixels(0,0,1,1) — because the GPU runs a frame or more behind the CPU, so a bracket with no sync point measures how fast commands were queued, not how long they took. performance.now() is clamped to 1 ms in Firefox and WebKit; a figure marked † there is a bound, not a value. WebGL's own GPU timer, EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2, is not used: it ships disabled or coarsened over timing-attack concerns, so the instrument here is coarser than the timestamp-query slide 14 will have — and that asymmetry is part of the difference between the two APIs, not an excuse for it. §1

Four small experiences

Each of these is a page an arts organisation could ship, because the asset is public and the picture is computed. An auction house's lot, a museum's object of the month, a conservation studio's examination bench, a museum's homepage — built on a CC0 photogrammetry scan, an Open Access painting and one authored relief, each drawn by a different program: a height field lit by a light you hold; a mesh with a normal map under a rig you switch; a painting read through a glass loupe that actually refracts; a collection assembling from a hundred and twenty thousand points that never leave the GPU. None of them is a picture of the thing. Each is the thing, evaluated.

§2

Structural colour

The colour is stored nowhere, because it is computed from the angle. An abalone shell, a jewel beetle, a soap bubble, oil on a puddle and a compact disc have no pigment for the colours they show. Each is a structure a few hundred nanometres across that sorts white light by wavelength according to where you stand — and each sorts it by a different mechanism. This is an encyclopaedia of those mechanisms. Every plate is computed per pixel from the geometry, the light and your eye, by the physics the mechanism column states, and you can turn every one of them.

Instrument: as §1 — performance.now() closed by a readPixels of the surface that was drawn, median of 9 after 3 warm frames, floor printed above. Control: the beetle's plate multiplies the number of layers in its transfer-matrix stack, 1 → 4 → 12, at constant geometry — every pixel walks one 2×2 complex matrix per layer per wavelength per polarisation, so the per-pixel work scales with the layers and nothing else does. The other plates sweep raster area, the control a blind clock cannot fake. The hue shift is the beetle model's own, from the same function that shades it: the peak of the unpolarised reflectance at the crown, at 0° against 10° and at 30° against 40°. The literature gives the direction — shorter with incidence, indices 1.6–1.7 — and this book holds itself to the direction; it does not claim a cited magnitude.

§3

The poster is a program

The artwork has no resolution, because it is a function, not a file. A studio builds an identity system for a concert hall's season and hands it over; the client runs it. What the client holds is a rack: a seed, a speed, an energy, and four programs that draw the same event four different ways — an ink field the title is pulled through, a bundle of threads in depth, the title cast as relief under a travelling light, and a mark of particles that the CPU has to feed every frame. Evaluated at 7016 × 9933 any of them is the A2. Turn the seed.

Figure 1, resolution. The Ink program — the print master — evaluated offscreen at this stage's size, at 4× its area, and at A2 at 150 dpi, bracketed to a readPixels of the render target itself (reading the screen's framebuffer waits only for the screen's commands, and an offscreen render can still be running when that returns — measured here, 17 Mpx read as free). Area is the control the canvas track proved a blind clock cannot fake. The ms-per-megapixel column says what the print will cost. Figure 2, the transfer — the number slide 14 prints its zero against. The Particles program integrates — particles on the CPU and uploads them every frame; WebGL has no compute stage to keep them on the GPU. The bracket opens before the upload and closes at a named sync point: a fenceSync polled with clientWaitSync inside the same task, or — since Chromium updates fence status only between tasks — readPixels, and the sheet prints which. Control: bytes per frame, by padding the vertex stride at constant particle count, up to 31× the real frame's bytes; 248 B is the widest stride WebGL allows. The integration and the draw do not change; only the bytes do. The particle count is the contract with §3 of slide 14, which re-runs it with the CPU out of the loop.

What this sheet is holding itself to

  1. Name the instrument, and its floor. Every printed figure carries the method that produced it. Where the instrument cannot resolve a value the sheet prints a bound, never a zero.
  2. A timing bracket needs a sync point. The GPU runs behind the CPU by design; readPixels is the wall it cannot run past.
  3. A control must vary the thing the instrument might be missing. Lights for the relief, raster area for the mesh and the loupe, point count for the hero — each moves one kind of work and nothing else.
  4. Strictly WebGL, with help. Three.js is the dependency: its renderer, loader, materials and environment lighting. Where a program is the experience it is a RawShaderMaterial, written out, so the room can read it.
  5. Only the subject is shaded. Cover the object and nothing that remains — frame, rule, leader, label — has a highlight, a bevel or a shadow.

Assets, and what is invented

The scan, the painting and the shell are public. Marble Bust 01 is a Poly Haven photogrammetry scan, CC0. Young Woman with a Water Pitcher is Johannes Vermeer, ca. 1662, from The Metropolitan Museum of Art's Open Access programme, CC0, Marquand Collection, 1889. The abalone is the Khronos glTF sample asset, © Abby Crawford, materials Eric Chadwick, CC BY 4.0. Licences and links are recorded beside the files in design/assets/LICENCES.md, and each plate prints its line.

The beetle, the bubble, the slick, the disc and the poster are authored, and their physics is written out: a transfer-matrix stack, a single film, a grating equation, a warped field. Chrysochroa fulgidissima is a real species; its shape here is not a scan. Lantern Hall does not exist.

The medal is invented — cast in code when the page loads, twelve named operations into one array. The businesses are invented: no Haldane & Rowe, no Fenwick, no Studio 4; the bust's accession number and the examination date are fiction. The measurements are real, taken on this machine when this page loaded.
