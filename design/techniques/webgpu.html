SL-14

webgpu

The GPU stops being a place you send pictures to and becomes a place things happen. Compute passes read and write storage buffers that never come back; a hundred thousand grains can be an interface, ink and smoke and a drink can be solved rather than faked, a flock can be an experiment, a world can be grown and flown over, and a product shot can be lit without a product. Six things WebGPU is for, each shown as a fragment of a product that could ship — because a technique on a coloured square teaches nothing about where you would reach for it.

Every number on this sheet is measured on this machine, now. The GPU writes its own clock at the start and end of each pass (timestamp-query), so cards print passes, not frames. Pipeline

what a frame costs on the GPU

A frame here is a list of passes. Each compute pass runs one program across a grid of workgroups; the render pass draws. Validation happens once, when the pipeline is created, so a pass costs what its work costs and nothing else — and because the GPU timestamps the start and end of every pass, the cost is a measurement rather than a guess. Switch passes off and watch the bars: the scene loses the pass's effect and the frame loses exactly its time.

Storage buffers · compute

particles and pixels as interface

The oldest WebGPU demo is a particle system, and it is usually a screensaver. It is more useful as an interface: when a hundred thousand grains live in a storage buffer and a compute pass moves every one of them each frame, text can be made of sand, a refresh can be a swarm, a switch can pour, a hero can part around the hand — and the CPU sends nothing per frame but the pointer. And when the motion is the brand rather than the data, the card itself can be the program: a wallet of eight business cards, each carrying its company's real mark, alive.

Wallet · on the iPhone Duo, open

Eight invented issuers, one wallet, drawn on the Duo's inner display at one CSS pixel per point: the stack on the left leaf, one card always live on the right with its details and a side rail, because iOS 27 puts controls on the edges and nothing rests on the crease. Tap a card in the stack and the two exchange — opposite arcs across the fold, 640 ms, the touched card in front — which is the one event a single screen cannot stage. The printed block is identical on every card and never moves; above it each card runs one process: Orrery's brass rings under a sweeping key light; Rosette's blood cells drifting in depth; Syringa's quatrefoil pumping violet dye at sixty beats a minute; Vesper Line's night train seen only as the light it leaves; Vitrine's glass stopper throwing a caustic; Mullion's louvred wall turning in a wave; Halation's thirty thousand points settling into volumes; Busbar's grid where charge hops one link per tick.

Every company, person, mark and scheme is invented; the marks are drawn in code. The device geometry is Apple's published 164.6 × 117.8 mm open, 1878 × 2670 inner panel — see conductor/tracks/004-webgpu-sl-14/wallet-duo.md for what is verified and what is constructed. In the stack each card is a still; its process runs on the right leaf. Left on auto, the wallet exchanges every fourteen seconds; the rail steps by hand.

Fluids · 2-D grids · 3-D volumes · particles

things that pour, drift and write

A fluid is the one effect a designer asks for and a front-end team says no to. In WebGL it is a texture ping-pong at whatever resolution a quad can afford. In compute it is the natural shape of the work — a grid or a particle set in a storage buffer, a few passes a frame, as many as the frame can pay for — and that makes three kinds of fluid practical on a page: a stable solve on paper, a volume you can walk light through, and particles that behave like a drink.

Atomics · sorting · indirect draw

populations big enough to be the content

Past a few thousand agents, the hard part is not moving them — it is letting them find each other. A scatter write (this bird counts itself into that cell) needs atomics; sorting by cell needs a writable buffer; both are things a fragment shader cannot do. With them, a quarter of a million agents can each consult their neighbours every frame, and a simulation becomes something a researcher or a game can put a question to.

Procedural generation

a world grown, not drawn

A downloaded map is a picture; a generated one is a process, and a process can be re-run with one number changed. Compute passes write the heights from noise, route water downhill across them and carve where it runs; a ray march then draws the result with real light — and because the whole world is in a buffer, going somewhere is a camera move rather than a page load. Five places, one world, and the site is the map.

What this sheet is holding itself to

  1. Name the pass, not the frame. Every GPU time printed here is one named pass between two timestamps.
  2. The zero is queried. Bytes per frame come from a counter on the device queue; the pointer and the uniforms are what crosses, and they are printed rather than rounded away.
  3. A readback is a stall with a number. Where a card asks the GPU a question — a fragment count, five heights — it prints what the asking cost.
  4. Raw WGSL, no library. The storage buffers, the workgroups, the atomics and the sort are the argument.
  5. Every product is invented. Atlas, Nectar, the eight issuers in the wallet and their scheme, Tinta, Fume, Pour, the paper and its authors, Tessera and Halcyon do not exist. The iPhone Duo is Apple's; the model's dimensions are labelled verified, derived or constructed on the card. The physics, the rule after Ballerini et al. 2008 and every measurement are real.
