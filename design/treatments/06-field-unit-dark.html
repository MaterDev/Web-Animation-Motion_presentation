what changed

T-05 → T-06

Every component rule is identical to T-05 — nine materials, twelve micro-animations, the glass optics, the Liquid Glass morph/tilt/concentric set. This fork is tokens only, and the interesting part is that not all of them were a straight swap.

Housing and tints are T-01's, not T-05's inverted. T-05 inherited light-tuned tints from T-02 — lower lightness, higher chroma, built to hold against a bright ground. Flipping the base to dark without also flipping the tint formula makes every section colour read muddy. T-06 uses T-01's dark-tuned set instead: higher lightness, so colour pops off near-black the way T-05's popped off near-white.
Elevation needed no changes at all. DESIGN.md's original rule — shadow reads as smudge on dark, so raised surfaces get lighter instead — turns out to already be built into T-05's component CSS. Every plate and mat gradient runs hz-200 → hz-100 top to bottom, and on this ramp hz-200 actually is lighter than hz-100. The outer drop shadows in --lift-* fade to near-invisible against a dark ground, which is correct, not broken — the inset top-edge highlight was always doing the real work.
Three recipes used absolute lightness and had to be retuned by hand. Rubber (.mat-rubber) and the grille bore (.mat-grille) were anchored at a fixed L≈0.30–0.44 — darker than T-05's L≈0.94 aluminium, so they read as recessed and light-absorbing. Against this ramp's L≈0.22–0.28 aluminium, that same fixed value is lighter than the metal around it: a black overmould rendering pale, a drilled hole reading as a raised stud. Both are now anchored near-black instead of at a constant, so they stay the darkest thing in the kit regardless of scheme.
The LCD well had the same problem. --well-floor sat at L 0.235 — clearly a recess below T-05's 0.94 chassis, but almost exactly inside this ramp's 0.14–0.28 range, so a display well stopped reading as a cavity. Dropped to L 0.085, below the darkest chassis surface in either scheme.
The rule this generalises to: a token pair is safe to fork by inversion only if both sides are defined relative to the ramp. Anything written as a bare oklch(0.44 …) instead of var(--hz-200) is a light-mode assumption hiding in a dark-mode-agnostic-looking rule, and it will not survive a fork unless it's checked by hand — this is exactly how three of them got missed on the first pass through this file.

Usability pass — T-06 as the kept base

T-06 is now the only treatment carried forward; the rest are archived, not deleted (still on disk in design/treatments/, just off the switcher). Everything below was found and fixed against SCOPE.md/DESIGN.md's own stated commitments, verified live in the browser rather than assumed from source.

Material labels were unreadable — fixed. .mat .tag/.mat .use pill backgrounds were a hardcoded near-white (oklch(1 0 0 / 0.78)) inherited unchanged from T-05, paired with text that correctly followed the ramp (--hz-800) — near-white on near-white. Measured by sampling actual rendered pixels rather than judging by eye: roughly 1.3:1 contrast, affecting 10 of 12 labelled tiles. The working dark-pill treatment that only two tiles had (.dark) is now the base rule for all of them; the now-redundant modifier was removed rather than left as a dead, misleading class.
Two disconnected reduced-motion controls — merged. The presenter unit's "Reduced" button had no id and no handler; #toggleRM in the motion section was the only one that worked. Both now read and write one shared body.rm state, and the engaged state is visible on either button (.ibtn[aria-pressed='true']), not just present in the DOM.
Four missing features restored. Progress rail and overview grid (both in DESIGN.md's Feature Set table — must-have and should-have respectively), the slide-code indicator DESIGN.md calls "the highest-leverage detail for the aesthetic," and the pacing-vs-budget LCD (--dur aside, the actual must-have line item is "elapsed, per-slide, and pacing") — all present through T-04, all quietly dropped when T-05 rebuilt this sheet around materials and motion. See §08 and the presenter unit above.
Keyboard story, decided per-control rather than left implicit. The encoder, toggle, and morph were <span>/<div> — confirmed by literally pressing Tab and watching focus skip over them, not by reading the markup — now real <button>s with the correct role/state (role="switch", aria-expanded). The specular-tilt panel deliberately did not get a keyboard path: it's ambient material response, not a control, and forcing one on would have been decoration, not access.

"Aluminium" is not one finish — it's a tool direction. Anodised brush runs linear, turned runs concentric, milled leaves parallel channels. Same metal, same colour, and they read as completely different parts. Anodised dye is the one that earns its place functionally: the dye sinks into the oxide layer so the grain still shows through, which is exactly what separates anodising from paint — and it means accent parts can carry --tint while still reading as metal.
The chamfer is the highest-value detail here. A bright hairline around a machined edge is the single most recognisable Teenage Engineering signature, and it costs one inset ring plus a directional highlight. Applied to the hero panel above.
Material must follow function or it's costume. Anodised for the chassis, blasted for secondary panels, knurled only where a hand would grip, rubber only where the unit touches a surface. Applied decoratively, six materials look worse than one.
02

glass

Fig. 2

Earlier sheets rejected "glassmorphism" and that rejection stands — for the flat version. Real glass does four things the cliché doesn't: it refracts what's behind it, lenses hardest at the rim, throws caustics along the edge, and disperses colour. All four are below. Hover the fourth.

01 · Flat blur
The cliché. One blur, one even border.
02 · Optical
Rim lensing, caustics, dispersion. CSS only.
03 · Refractive
Backdrop actually displaced. Grid bends.
04 · Liquid
Hover — radius morphs, body swells.
The grid behind the panels is the test. Straight rules are the only honest way to show refraction: under 01 they stay straight and merely blur, under 03 they bend. If a glass treatment can't visibly distort a straight line, it isn't glass — it's a translucent rectangle.
Glass needs a subject, and that's the real cost. Every panel here sits on a deliberately busy bed. Over flat grey it collapses back into the cliché no matter how good the optics are. For this deck that's actually fine — the demos are behind the chrome — but it means glass can only be used where something is genuinely running underneath, never as a default panel style.
Cost check. backdrop-filter forces the compositor to re-sample everything beneath, and the SVG displacement doubles that. Three of these on screen is fine; one over a live WebGPU particle sim is not. Rule for the build: glass never overlaps a running demo — it goes on chrome that sits beside one.
03

liquid glass motion

Fig. 3 · Apple, WWDC 2025

Not water physics — Apple's actual system, shipped as "Liquid Glass" across iOS/macOS/iPadOS 26. Sources: Apple Newsroom, MacStories. It has almost nothing to do with the blob/ripple/tank build that was here before this pass — that was a literal reading of "liquid" that doesn't match what Apple built. Four things define the real system, and only the last one overlaps with what was here already.

A · Morph
Trigger click · 420ms · liquid
The control becomes the panel — width, height and radius animate together, nothing is swapped or replaced. Apple: "the element expands organically rather than just moving."
B · Specular tilt — move your cursor over it
Trigger pointer move · continuous
Highlight tracks motion in real time — device accelerometer on hardware, cursor position standing in for it here. Not a fixed gradient; the light genuinely moves with you.
Keyboard: deliberately none. This is ambient material response layered on top of other content, not a control — nothing here is only reachable this way, so unlike the knob/toggle/morph it doesn't need a keyboard path; it already has a sensible static rest state.
C · Concentric radius
Formula r = outer − padding, always
Every nested radius is computed, never eyeballed — calc(var(--r) - var(--p)). A fixed inner radius next to a rounder outer one is the single most common tell of a UI that copied the look without the system.
D · Spring indicator
Trigger click · 420ms · liquid
Resists leaving, catches up, settles without oscillating. The one place the earlier build was already right — Apple's motion is spring-based throughout, and --ease-liquid already approximated that correctly.
What survives from the first pass: exactly one curve. --ease-liquid and the spring/settle idea behind it were correct — Apple's system really is spring-driven. Everything built around that curve was wrong: metaball fusion, a contact ripple, and a liquid-filled tank are water-simulation ideas with no counterpart in Liquid Glass. They've been removed rather than kept as extras, so this section doesn't quietly teach the wrong thing.
Where morph does not belong. Slide advance, for the same reason as before — it happens 200+ times before delivery, and a shape-morph at that frequency is the exact failure mode SCOPE.md names. Morph is for moments of arrival: a control opening into its own detail, not routine navigation.

Trigger value change · 180ms · mechanical
Real LCD segments drag as they switch. One frame of blur and 1px offset — the detail that makes a readout feel physical.

CurveValueUse
--ease-standard0.2,0,0,1Default. Slide advance, most state change.
--ease-decelerate0.05,0.7,0.1,1Entering. Things arrive gently.

--ease-detent0.34,1.28,0.64,1New. Sprung mechanisms only — encoder, toggle. Slight overshoot.

--ease-liquidlinear(…)New. Surface tension — merge, level, elastic indicator. Authored as linear(); a bezier can't carry the inflections.
Seven curves is the ceiling, and this is it. Past this a motion system stops being a system — nobody holds eight curves in their head and they start getting picked by feel. Liquid earned the seventh slot because linear() does something no bezier here can. An eighth means one of these is wrong.
